Lifestyle creep is what happens when spending rises to meet income. A 10% raise absorbed by a slightly bigger apartment, a newer car and better coffee leaves you with exactly the same month you had before — and no more security.

## What absorbing a raise costs

Starting at $60,000 take-home with 4% annual raises:

| Behaviour | Year 1 extra | Year 5 extra | Year 10 extra | 10-year total kept |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Absorb all of it | $2,400 | $12,988 | $28,117 | $0 |
| Save half | $1,200 | $6,494 | $14,059 | ~$74,000 |
| Save 75% | $1,800 | $9,741 | $21,088 | ~$111,000 |
| Save it all | $2,400 | $12,988 | $28,117 | ~$148,000 |

The first-year difference between absorbing the raise and saving it is $2,400 — small. Over ten years the same behaviour is the difference between nothing and roughly $148,000 of accumulated savings, before any investment return.

## Where the money goes

| Creep category | Typical monthly increase | Annual cost | Work hours at $25/h |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bigger rent or mortgage | $200 – $600 | $2,400 – $7,200 | 96 – 288 |
| Newer car or higher payment | $100 – $300 | $1,200 – $3,600 | 48 – 144 |
| More eating out and delivery | $80 – $250 | $960 – $3,000 | 38 – 120 |
| New subscriptions and upgrades | $20 – $80 | $240 – $960 | 10 – 38 |
| Better holidays | $50 – $200 | $600 – $2,400 | 24 – 96 |
| **Common total** | **$450 – $1,430** | **$5,400 – $17,160** | **216 – 686** |

A $500 raise a month feels like it should change your life. Spread across five categories, it changes nothing you would notice — which is precisely how it disappears.

## The mechanism

1. **Raises arrive as monthly amounts, not annual ones.** $416 a month looks small and gets absorbed by three small decisions.
2. **Fixed costs are sticky.** A lease or car payment signed after a raise locks the raise in for years.
3. **Comparison shifts.** New colleagues, new neighbourhood, new baseline.
4. **Nothing forces a decision.** Without an automatic transfer, the default is to spend it.

## Four ways to keep most of a raise

**1. Move it before you see it.** Increase your savings standing order by the full raise amount on the day it lands. Spending you never see is spending you do not miss.

**2. Take a fixed percentage, not a fixed amount.** 50% of every raise going to savings scales automatically and survives future raises.

**3. Allow one deliberate upgrade.** Pick the single change that improves your life most, cap it at a fraction of the raise, and let the rest go to savings. Total abstention is not sustainable; unlimited creep is not saving.

**4. Delay the fixed costs.** Do not sign a new lease, car payment or contract in the first month of a raise. Six months of banking the difference tells you whether you actually want the upgrade.

| Strategy | Saved from a $400/mo raise | Annual | Five years |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Move it all on payday | $400/mo | $4,800 | $24,000 |
| Half, plus one capped upgrade of $100 | $300/mo | $3,600 | $18,000 |
| 25% of every raise | $100/mo | $1,200 | $6,000 |
| Absorb it | $0 | $0 | $0 |

## When spending more is right

Not all increases are creep. Spending more is sensible when it removes a real problem:

| Increase | Creep or improvement? |
| --- | --- |
| Shorter commute after a house move | Improvement — buys back hours |
| Childcare that enables work | Improvement — it is a cost of earning |
| Paying off high-interest debt | Improvement — a guaranteed return |
| Health, dental or therapy costs | Improvement |
| A bigger car because colleagues have one | Creep |
| More expensive coffee because you can | Creep |
| A larger apartment you do not use | Creep |

The test: does it reduce a cost you were already paying in money, time or stress? If not, it is likely creep.
