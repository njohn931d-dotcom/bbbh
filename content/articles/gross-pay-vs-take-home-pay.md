**Gross pay** is what your employer agrees to pay you. **Take-home pay** is what reaches your bank account after tax, social contributions and deductions. The gap between them is often 20% to 40%, and it is the reason a salary figure can be a misleading way to think about affordability.

## What typically comes off

| Deduction | Usually | Varies by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Income tax | Progressive bands | Country, region, allowances |
| Social security or national insurance | A percentage up to a cap | Country, earnings ceiling |
| Pension or retirement contribution | A fixed percentage | Your election and any employer match |
| Health, dental or other benefits | Fixed premium | Employer plan |
| Union dues or professional fees | Fixed | Membership |
| Student loan or similar repayments | Percentage above a threshold | Repayment plan |

Some deductions come out before income tax is calculated (often called pre-tax or salary-sacrifice), which reduces taxable pay — a useful distinction when comparing two job offers with the same headline salary.

| $40,000 | $32,000 | $28,800 | $26,000 | $22,000 |
| $52,000 | $41,600 | $37,440 | $33,800 | $28,600 |
| $60,000 | $48,000 | $43,200 | $39,000 | $33,000 |
| $80,000 | $64,000 | $57,600 | $52,000 | $44,000 |
| $100,000 | $80,000 | $72,000 | $65,000 | $55,000 |

These are flat illustrative rates. Real tax systems are progressive, so the effective rate usually rises with income.

## Monthly and hourly versions

At $60,000 gross, 2,080 hours a year:

| | Gross | Take-home at 28% off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Per year | $60,000 | $43,200 |
| Per month | $5,000 | $3,600 |
| Every two weeks | $2,307.69 | $1,661.54 |
| Per week | $1,153.85 | $830.77 |
| Per hour | $28.85 | $20.77 |

## The mistake this causes

If you price your spending in gross pay, every purchase looks cheaper than it is:

| Purchase | Hours at $28.85 gross | Hours at $20.77 take-home | Underestimate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $100 | 3.5 | 4.8 | 1.3 h |
| $500 | 17.3 | 24.1 | 6.8 h |
| $1,500 rent | 52.0 | 72.2 | 20.2 h |
| $5,400 car/year | 187.2 | 259.9 | 72.7 h |

A $1,500 rent looks like 52 hours of work in gross terms and is really 72 hours — a difference of two and a half working days a month. Any "hours of work" calculation should use take-home pay.

## How to find your own numbers

1. **Take-home:** add up the net amounts from your last 12 payslips, or work from a recent month and multiply — being careful with months that have bonuses, overtime or irregular deductions.
2. **Effective deduction rate:** `1 − (take-home ÷ gross)`. If you take home $43,200 of $60,000, the rate is 28%.
3. **Real hourly rate:** take-home ÷ hours actually worked, including commute and unpaid time. See [Your Real Hourly Wage](/articles/pay-and-rates/your-real-hourly-wage/).

## Gross vs take-home: which to use where

| Use gross pay for | Use take-home pay for |
| --- | --- |
| Comparing job offers | Budgeting |
| Checking which tax band you are in | Deciding what you can afford |
| Working out a percentage raise | Converting prices to hours of work |
| Contract and invoice rates | Savings rates and debt repayment |

## When gross and net move differently

Two things confuse people comparing pay over time:

- **A raise can push part of your income into a higher band** without reducing take-home pay; only the portion above the threshold is taxed at the higher rate.
- **Pre-tax deductions reduce taxable pay**, so increasing a pension contribution can raise take-home pay by less than the contribution amount.
