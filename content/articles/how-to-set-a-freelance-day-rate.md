A freelance day rate is not your old salary divided by 260. It has to cover the days you do not bill, the overheads your employer used to pay, and the tax and benefits you now fund yourself.

**Day rate = (target take-home + overheads + tax and benefit provision) ÷ billable days per year**

## Step 1: count your billable days

Start with 52 weeks, then subtract everything that is not a paid working day:

| Deduction | Typical days |
| --- | --- |
| Weeks in a year | 260 weekdays |
| Public holidays | −8 to −12 |
| Vacation | −20 to −25 |
| Sick and personal days | −5 to −10 |
| Admin, invoicing, business development | −40 to −60 |
| **Billable days remaining** | **~155 to 185** |

Most freelancers bill far fewer days than they imagine. Assuming 220 billable days is the most common early mistake — and it underprices the rate by 20–30%.

## Step 2: set your income target

Work backwards from what you need to take home, then add the costs of being in business.

| Item | Annual amount |
| --- | --- |
| Target take-home pay | (your number) |
| Tax and social contributions | +25% to 45% of gross |
| Pension or retirement | +5% to 10% |
| Health or income protection cover | +$1,200 to $6,000 |
| Software, hardware, insurance | +$1,000 to $4,000 |
| Accountant, bank fees, professional bodies | +$500 to $2,500 |
| **Required gross revenue** | **sum of the above** |

## Step 3: divide

At a $60,000 target take-home:

| | Conservative | Mid | Optimistic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Target take-home | $60,000 | $60,000 | $60,000 |
| Tax and contributions (32%) | $28,235 | $28,235 | $28,235 |
| Pension (8%) | $7,059 | $7,059 | $7,059 |
| Overheads | $7,000 | $4,000 | $2,500 |
| **Gross revenue needed** | **$102,294** | **$99,294** | **$97,794** |
| Billable days | 155 | 170 | 185 |
| **Day rate** | **$660** | **$584** | **$529** |

Round up, not down: $650 rather than $660, and never to a figure that leaves no room for a client to negotiate.

## Hourly vs day rate vs project fee

| Model | Pros | Cons | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Day rate | Simple, covers scope creep | Client compares to salary | Ongoing or unclear work |
| Hourly | Precise for small tasks | Punishes efficiency | Short, variable tasks |
| Project fee | Client loves certainty | Risk sits with you | Clearly defined deliverables |
| Retainer | Predictable income | Scope can expand | Ongoing monthly work |

A common conversion: **day rate ÷ 8 = hourly rate**, then add 20–30% for half-day work, because half days cost you a full day in practice. Compare the result with the pay you are giving up using [Gross Pay vs Take-Home Pay](/articles/pay-and-rates/gross-pay-vs-take-home-pay/), and build a buffer with [How Big Should My Emergency Fund Be?](/articles/saving-habits/how-big-should-my-emergency-fund-be/) — freelancers usually need more months of cover than employees.

## Five checks before you quote

1. **Does it survive a bad year?** Test the rate at 120 billable days.
2. **Does it beat employment?** Compare to a salaried role including pension, paid leave, equipment and sick pay.
3. **Does it cover the unbilled 40%?** Admin, sales and invoicing are real work.
4. **Is there room to negotiate?** Quote 10–15% above your floor.
5. **Does it allow for a rate rise?** Plan to raise rates annually; inflation and experience both compound.

## Raising your rate

| Trigger | Suggested increase |
| --- | --- |
| Every 12 months | 5 – 10% |
| Cost of living rise | Match inflation or your cost increase |
| New specialism or certification | 10 – 20% |
| Portfolio and demand outgrow capacity | 15 – 25% |

Give existing clients 30 to 60 days' notice in writing. Clients who leave over a 7% rise were usually the ones keeping you from raising it.

Assumptions: flat illustrative tax and pension rates, no business rates or local taxes, no employees. Tax treatment, social contributions and allowable expenses vary by country. General information, not tax or financial advice.
