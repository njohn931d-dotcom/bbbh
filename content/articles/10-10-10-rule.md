The 10/10/10 rule is a decision shortcut from decision strategist Suzie Orloff's framework: before choosing, ask how you'll feel about this decision in **10 minutes, 10 months and 10 years**. Applied to purchases, it drags a hot, present-biased decision through your future self's perspective — which is where most impulse spending quietly dies.

## How it works

Impulse purchases live entirely in the "10 minutes" column: the thrill of clicking, the relief of scratching the itch. The other columns are blank by default — the brain doesn't volunteer them. The rule forces you to fill them in:

- **10 minutes:** how will I feel right after buying? (Usually: excited — that's the column that's already winning.)
- **10 months:** will this still be used, or is it a story about a phase I had? Will the bill feel large or trivial? Will I be glad, indifferent or annoyed?
- **10 years:** will I remember this purchase at all? Did it change anything — a habit, a skill, a comfort — or is the money simply gone?

The decision isn't automatically "no" — it's *informed*. Some purchases score beautifully on all three horizons (the good mattress, the trip with friends, the tool you use weekly); others collapse the moment the ten-month question appears.

## Worked examples

**$85 jacket, sale ends tonight:**
- 10 min: great — it fits and it's 40% off.
- 10 months: probably worn 6–8 times; the "sale" price was normal price last season; the other jackets still exist.
- 10 years: invisible. *Verdict: pass, add to the 30-day list; the sale pressure itself is a red flag.*

**$600 course in your field:**
- 10 min: nervous — committing money and evenings.
- 10 months: if completed, likely changes my rates or my confidence at work.
- 10 years: career investments compound; this one's trajectory is upward. *Verdict: buy — possibly after checking the refund window.*

**$45 dinner out with old friends:**
- 10 min: looking forward to it.
- 10 months: I'll remember the conversation, not the money.
- 10 years: the friendship is the asset; dinners are how they're maintained. *Verdict: go, without guilt.*

Notice the pattern: the rule targets *reflexive* spend, not deliberate ones. Guilt-free approval of things that pass all three horizons is a feature, not a bug.

## Using it in the moment

1. **Name the three horizons out loud or in a note** — writing prevents the 10-minute feeling from answering for all three.
2. **Set a default pause** for anything above your threshold; the rule works best when the cart waits an hour or a day.
3. **Log one-line verdicts.** Over time you'll see your own failure pattern: "always approves in 10-month column at work stress" or "ten-year column never saves a subscription."
4. **Pair it with the money-unit check:** convert to hours of your take-home pay — 10/10/10 handles time-preference, hours-of-life handles price salience. Together they cover the two main impulse traps.

## Where it struggles

Genuinely urgent decisions (the car repair today) don't need horizons — they need action. And decision-fatigued evenings are exactly when the 10-minute voice gets loudest — so run the rule while fresh, or defer the purchase to morning; the morning version of the rule is almost always the honest one.

## FAQ

### Who created the 10/10/10 rule?
It's associated with author and decision coach Suzie Orloff (popularized in her book *10/10/10*), and it's used broadly in personal and business decision-making. The finance application is a straightforward adaptation: run your purchases through the same three horizons.

### Does the rule work for big life decisions, not spending?
Yes — jobs, moves, relationships. That's its original domain. For purchases it's simply a compact version of the same test: what does my future self think of today's self?

### How is this different from the 24-hour rule?
The 24-hour rule adds *time*; 10/10/10 adds *perspective*. Time alone just delays a bad decision — plenty of carts still get checked out on day two. The horizons give the waiting period something to do.

### Won't this make me too cautious to enjoy money?
Only if you answer the 10-year question dishonestly. Experiences, gifts and well-chosen comfort pass it easily. If the rule starts blocking everything, you've turned a scalpel into a club — review verdicts monthly and re-approve the categories that genuinely matter to you.
