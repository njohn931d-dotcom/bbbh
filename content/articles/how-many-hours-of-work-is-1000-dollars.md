Divide $1,000 by your take-home hourly pay. At $25 an hour, $1,000 is **40 hours of work** — a full working week. At $15 an hour it is 66.7 hours, and at $100 an hour it is 10 hours.

That is the whole calculation. The tables below do it for the rates people search for most.

## $1,000 in work hours by hourly pay

| Take-home hourly pay | Hours of work for $1,000 | 8-hour workdays |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $15 | 66.7 | 8.3 days |
| $20 | 50 | 6.3 days |
| $25 | 40 | 5 days |
| $30 | 33.3 | 4.2 days |
| $40 | 25 | 3.1 days |
| $50 | 20 | 2.5 days |
| $75 | 13.3 | 1.7 days |
| $100 | 10 | 1.3 days |

## The number the price tag hides: gross earnings needed

If you are paid take-home money but quoted a pre-tax price, you must earn more than the price to afford it. The gross amount you need is:

```
Gross earnings needed = price ÷ (1 − effective tax rate)
```

| Effective tax + deductions | Gross you must earn to spend $1,000 |
| --- | --- |
| 15% | $1,176 |
| 22% | $1,282 |
| 28% | $1,389 |
| 35% | $1,538 |
| 45% | $1,818 |

At a 28% effective rate, a "$1,000 purchase" is really $1,389 of earnings — about 55.6 hours of work at a $25 take-home rate, not 40.

## One-off versus recurring

A single $1,000 purchase is one week of work. A $1,000 *annual* habit is a week of work every year, forever:

| Recurring cost | Per month | Per year | Work hours at $25/h |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $1,000/year | $83.33 | $1,000 | 40 h |
| $1,000/year for 5 years | $83.33 | $5,000 total | 200 h |
| $1,000/year for 10 years | $83.33 | $10,000 total | 400 h |

Frequency matters far more than amount. A $1,000 one-off is a decision; $1,000 a year is a commitment worth ten weeks of your life over a decade.

## How to run your own number in 20 seconds

1. Find your take-home pay for one month.
2. Divide by the hours you work in a month (173.33 if you work 40 hours a week).
3. Divide the price by that hourly figure.

Or open the [cost-of-time calculator](/calculators/cost-of-time/) and enter the price and your pay — it does the same arithmetic, including the hourly, monthly and annual inputs.

## Why your answer may differ from a friend's

Two people can look at the same $1,000 laptop and see 40 hours and 8 hours of work. Neither is wrong. The calculation measures *your* trade-off, which is the only one you have to live with.

Assumptions: take-home pay, 8-hour workdays, no changes in price or pay over the period. Investment returns, inflation and taxes beyond the stated effective rate are not modelled.
