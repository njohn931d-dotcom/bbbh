Two purchases are only comparable on price if they last the same amount of time. **Cost per use = price ÷ number of times you use it.** A $150 pair of shoes worn 150 times costs $1 per wear; a $60 pair worn 30 times costs $2 per wear — the "cheaper" pair is twice as expensive.

## The formula

```
Cost per use = total cost of ownership ÷ number of uses
Cost per year = total cost of ownership ÷ years of life
Total cost of ownership = purchase price + running costs − resale value
```

## Worked comparisons

| Purchase | Price | Expected uses | Cost per use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Running shoes, premium | $160 | 500 km ≈ 100 runs | $1.60 |
| Running shoes, budget | $70 | 300 km ≈ 60 runs | $1.17 |
| Winter coat, quality | $300 | 8 winters × 60 days | $0.63 |
| Winter coat, fast fashion | $90 | 1 winter × 40 days | $2.25 |
| Coffee machine | $180 | 3 years × 250 cups | $0.24 + pods |
| Cafe coffee | $5 | 1 | $5.00 |
| Power drill | $120 | 40 projects over 10 years | $3.00 |
| Drill hire | $25/day | 1 day | $25.00 |
| Gym membership | $540/yr | 150 visits | $3.60 |
| Gym membership | $540/yr | 12 visits | $45.00 |

The pattern: **cost per use collapses with frequency.** That is why the expensive version wins for things you use daily and loses for things you use twice.

## Break-even: how many uses does the pricier option need?

```
Break-even uses = (expensive price − cheap price) ÷ (cheap cost per use − expensive cost per use)
```

| Cheap option | Premium option | Premium needs | To win |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $60 shoes, 30 wears ($2.00) | $150 shoes, 1 wear = ? | 75 wears | Last twice as long |
| $90 coat, 40 wears ($2.25) | $300 coat, 1 wear = ? | 133 wears | Last 3.3× as long |
| $25/day drill hire | $120 drill | 5 days | More than 5 projects |
| $5 cafe coffee | $180 machine + $0.30 pods | 38 cups | 38 homemade coffees |

A $150 coat replaced every year for five years costs $750. A $300 coat that lasts five years costs $300. The premium option wins by 133 uses, which at 60 wears a winter is just over two years.

## Cost per year instead of cost per use

For things you use continuously rather than in discrete events, switch to cost per year:

| Item | Price | Years of life | Cost/year | Cost/month |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sofa, budget | $600 | 5 | $120 | $10.00 |
| Sofa, quality | $1,800 | 15 | $120 | $10.00 |
| Mattress, mid | $900 | 8 | $112.50 | $9.38 |
| Mattress, premium | $2,000 | 12 | $166.67 | $13.89 |
| Phone, mid-range | $499 | 3 | $166.33 | $13.86 |
| Phone, flagship | $999 | 4 | $249.75 | $20.81 |
| Washing machine | $750 | 11 | $68.18 | $5.68 |

Identical cost per year ($120) for two sofas at very different prices — the deciding factor becomes comfort, space and how long you intend to stay put, not the sticker.

## Include running costs

Price alone hides the cost of ownership:

| Item | Price | Running cost | 5-year total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Printer | $80 | $40 cartridges × 3/yr | $680 |
| Printer | $220 | $25 cartridges × 1/yr | $345 |
| Petrol car | $22,000 | ~$8,000/yr | $62,000 |
| Electric car | $32,000 | ~$5,500/yr | $59,500 |
| Cheap appliance | $300 | $90/yr electricity | $750 |
| Efficient appliance | $600 | $55/yr electricity | $875 |

Running-cost figures here are illustrative — use your own usage, energy prices and servicing costs. The structure is what matters: a lower purchase price with higher running costs is often the more expensive choice after two or three years.

## When cost per use is the wrong metric

- **One-off experiences.** A concert ticket has one use and that is the point.
- **Things you might not like.** Buying the cheap version first is sensible when you are unsure — commitment is a real risk.
- **Rapidly improving categories.** A laptop bought to last eight years will be obsolete in five; pay for the horizon you actually have.
- **When quality affects safety or health.** Helmets, car seats, tools and footwear are not the place to optimise the last 20%.
- **When the cheap option creates friction.** A tool you avoid using because it is unpleasant has a cost per use of infinity.

## A 30-second rule

Estimate how many times you will use it in a year, how many years it will last, then divide. If the number is under $1, the decision rarely deserves more thought. If it is over $10, sleep on it — see [Seven Questions Before a Big Purchase](/articles/spending-decisions/seven-questions-before-a-big-purchase/).

Assumptions: no resale value unless stated, no inflation, no financing costs. Frequency estimates are the reader's to make; overestimating use is the most common error.
