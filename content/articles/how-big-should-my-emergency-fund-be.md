The most common guideline is **three to six months of essential expenses**. The right end of that range depends on how stable your income is, how many people rely on it, and how quickly you could find work again.

The size is measured in *essential expenses*, not income — the money you would still spend if you stopped earning: housing, food, utilities, transport, insurance, medication, minimum debt payments.

## Size by situation

| Situation | Suggested months of essentials | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salaried, stable sector, no dependents | 3 | Income risk is low |
| Salaried, one income supporting a household | 6 | One income, multiple dependents |
| Freelance or contract | 6 – 9 | Income gaps are normal, not emergencies |
| Commission or seasonal income | 9 – 12 | Slow periods recur annually |
| Single-income household with dependents | 6 – 12 | No second earner to absorb a gap |
| Business owner, variable drawings | 9 – 12 | Business and personal risk overlap |
| Between jobs or on a fixed-term contract | 6 – 12 | The end date is known |

## Two phases: the buffer, then the fund

Going straight for $16,800 is where most people stall. Split it:

| Phase | Target | Purpose | Typical timeline |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1: Starter buffer | $500 – $1,000 | Absorbs a repair, a dentist bill, a shortfall | 4 – 12 weeks |
| 2: Full fund | 3 – 6 months of essentials | Covers job loss or illness | 12 – 36 months |

The starter buffer is what stops a $600 car repair from becoming credit card debt, which is exactly how a full fund never gets built. See [How to Save $1,000 Fast](/articles/saving-habits/how-to-save-1000-dollars-fast/) for phase one.

## Where to keep it

| Feature | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Instant access | An emergency is not planned a week ahead |
| Separate from your current account | Money you can see is money you spend |
| Insured up to your local protection limit | Check the scheme and limit where you live |
| No volatility | The fund must be the same size in a bad month as a good one |
| No notice period | Notice accounts delay access precisely when you need it |

An emergency fund is not an investment. Its job is to be boring, stable and available — which means accepting a low return in exchange for certainty.

## What counts as an emergency

| Usually yes | Usually no |
| --- | --- |
| Job loss or reduced hours | A sale you do not want to miss |
| Urgent medical or dental costs | A holiday, however well-deserved |
| Essential appliance or car failure | A non-essential upgrade |
| Unexpected travel for a family emergency | Routine maintenance you could have planned for |
| Emergency housing repair | Gifts |

The test: is it unexpected, necessary, and urgent? Two out of three is usually a [sinking fund](/articles/saving-habits/sinking-funds-explained/), not an emergency — and planning for those in advance is what keeps this fund intact.

## Building it alongside debt

| Debt type | Typical approach |
| --- | --- |
| High-interest revolving debt | Build a small buffer ($500–$1,000), then prioritise the debt |
| Low-interest or 0% debt | Split contributions between fund and debt |
| Student or mortgage debt | Keep building the fund; these are long-term, low-rate |
| Employer-matched retirement contribution | Take the match first; it usually beats everything else |

## Refill rules

If you use the fund, it becomes the priority again. Two habits help: pause non-essential saving until it is restored, and keep a written note of what it is for — the note is what makes "no" easy when a non-emergency comes along.

Assumptions: no interest, investment returns, inflation or tax treatment. Deposit insurance limits and unemployment protections vary by country. General information, not financial advice.
