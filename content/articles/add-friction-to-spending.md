Adding friction to spending means deliberately making purchases slightly harder — deleting apps, removing saved cards, requiring a walk to the bank — so that impulse purchases die in the extra steps instead of surviving them. It's the personal-finance version of a design principle: behavior follows the path of least resistance, so reshape the path, not the person.

## Friction beats willpower

Self-control is a finite, fluctuating resource; friction is constant. Every saved card, one-click checkout and push notification exists to remove steps between wanting and owning — the entire modern commerce stack is a friction-removal machine pointed at your wallet. When your only defense is "trying harder," you're defending with mood against engineering.

The research pattern is consistent: small increases in effort — a few extra clicks, a short walk, having to wait — reduce follow-through on many behaviors dramatically, because the impulse that motivated the purchase decays faster than the effort it takes to satisfy it. You're not fighting the urge; you're letting it expire.

## The friction menu (pick your three)

**Checkout friction**
- Remove saved cards from shopping sites and apps
- Turn off one-click buying everywhere
- Log out after every purchase so the next one requires credentials
- Disable push notifications from retailers; unsubscribe from promo email

**Access friction**
- Delete shopping apps; keep browsing on the slow device
- Move payment apps off the home screen or off the phone entirely
- Leave cards at home for "just looking" trips; take a fixed cash amount

**Time friction**
- Mandatory 24-hour hold on buys above your threshold (30 days for big ones)
- A written queue: the 30-day list captures, reviews, and only then allows
- No-purchase windows: evenings after 10 p.m., first week after payday (when spending energy is highest)

**Social friction**
- Tell someone the rule; make cart-abandonment reportable
- Joint approval above a set amount for shared accounts — not as permission, but as a pause button

## Match friction to your failure mode

- **Impulse app buyer:** app deletion + saved-card removal (the whole attack on one-tap checkout)
- **Stress spender:** notification off + 24-hour rule + comfort list (friction at the cue, alternative at the urge)
- **Sale hunter:** unsubscribe + price-history check + "wait for the sale to end" rule (scarcity timers lose their power when the email never arrives)
- **Subscription creep:** calendar cancel-dates + one-in-one-out for subscriptions

One friction per failure mode is enough. Ten frictions produce system abandonment.

## The other side: reduce friction on good behavior

The same principle runs in reverse — make the right moves *easier*:
- Automate the savings transfer on payday (frictionless virtuous spending)
- Put the workout clothes out; put the gym on the commute route
- Keep the grocery list app on the home screen; keep staples in the house so hunger doesn't order delivery
- Pre-schedule the weekly money check-in

Behavior design that only punishes spending is brittle; pairing removal of bad-path ease with addition of good-path ease is what makes the system durable.

## FAQ

### Isn't this just making life inconvenient?
That's the point — targeted inconvenience. You're not removing convenience generally; you're restoring a small amount of it *specifically* where the current friction is zero and the regret rate is high. People find that re-typing a card feels fine within a week; the purchases they no longer make feel better still.

### Won't I just override the friction?
Sometimes — friction reduces probability, it doesn't set it to zero. But every removed step eliminates a percentage of impulse completions, and the ones that survive all the steps are closer to deliberate. Think of it as filtering, not blocking.

### Does friction work for couples?
Excellently: a rule like "anything over $150 waits until the next money date" adds a scheduled pause rather than a veto — fewer confrontations, fewer heat-of-the-moment purchases, and decisions made with both calendars visible.

### What's the first piece of friction to add?
Remove saved card details from your highest-usage shopping site or app. It's a five-minute change that attacks the single most powerful conversion tool pointed at your spending — one-tap checkout.
