The latte factor argument says that small daily purchases, redirected into savings, add up to a meaningful sum. That is arithmetically true and behaviourally incomplete: $5 a day is $1,825 a year, but cutting it only helps if the money actually gets saved — and small cuts cannot fix a large fixed cost.

## What it gets right

| Daily amount | Per year | Work hours at $25/h | Over 10 years |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $2 | $730 | 29 | $7,300 |
| $4 | $1,460 | 58 | $14,600 |
| $5 | $1,825 | 73 | $18,250 |
| $7.50 | $2,737.50 | 110 | $27,375 |
| $10 | $3,650 | 146 | $36,500 |

Frequency is the engine. A $5 daily habit is 1,825 decisions a year, and automating it removes 1,825 opportunities to choose badly. That is the real mechanism — not the coffee.

## What it gets wrong

**1. It assumes the saving happens.** Cutting a purchase does not automatically move the money anywhere. Most people who stop buying coffee find the money absorbed by other spending within weeks.

**2. It targets the wrong order of magnitude.** Compare the annual totals:

| Lever | Annual effect | Effort | Realistic? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Skip daily $5 coffee | $1,825 | Daily willpower, forever | Hard to sustain |
| Refinance or renegotiate insurance | $300 – $900 | 30 minutes a year | Easy |
| Move $300/mo rent lower | $3,600 | One move | Hard but one-off |
| Cancel unused subscriptions | $240 – $960 | 30 minutes twice a year | Easy |
| Negotiate a 5% raise | $2,000 – $4,000 | One conversation | Medium |
| Reduce a $450/mo car payment to $300 | $1,800 | One decision at renewal | Medium |

A single 30-minute insurance call can beat three months of skipping coffee. The latte is the *easiest* lever, not the *largest* one.

**3. It ignores the value of the purchase.** If the coffee is how you see a friend, start your day, or leave the house, it is not $5 of caffeine — it is $5 of something you would otherwise pay more for.

**4. It lands badly on people with no slack.** Telling someone whose rent takes 55% of their pay to skip a $5 coffee is the wrong advice, delivered to the wrong line item.

## The version that works

| Step | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Measure one month of discretionary spending honestly |
| 2 | Rank the categories by annual total, not by visibility |
| 3 | Fix the top one or two — usually housing, transport or food |
| 4 | Automate the saving before cutting anything else |
| 5 | Use small daily cuts as the last 10%, not the first |

## Category ranking, typically

| Category | Typical annual spend | Monthly | Lever size |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Housing | $14,400 – $36,000 | $1,200 – $3,000 | Very large |
| Transport | $6,000 – $14,000 | $500 – $1,170 | Very large |
| Food (groceries + eating out) | $6,000 – $12,000 | $500 – $1,000 | Large |
| Insurance | $1,200 – $3,600 | $100 – $300 | Medium |
| Subscriptions | $360 – $2,400 | $30 – $200 | Medium |
| Coffee and snacks | $365 – $1,825 | $30 – $152 | Small |

Coffee is at the bottom of the list. It gets the attention because it is visible and daily, not because it is large.

## When small cuts are exactly right

- **You have no savings habit at all.** A $2-a-day automated transfer is a way to start; the amount matters less than the automation.
- **You have already optimised the big items.** Once housing and transport are settled, the small categories are what remain.
- **You want a fast, visible win.** Momentum is worth something. A [$5-a-day challenge](/articles/saving-habits/five-dollars-a-day-savings-challenge/) that you finish beats a housing plan you never execute.

## The honest summary

Small daily spending is worth measuring — and it is rarely the thing standing between you and your goals. Automate the saving first, attack the two largest categories second, and let the coffee be one of the last decisions rather than the first.
