How Much Should I Save Each Month? (Rules, Tables & Reality)

Compare the 50/30/20 rule, the 20% rule and pay-yourself-first by income, with monthly savings tables and what to do when none of them fit.

There is no universal number. The most useful starting point is a percentage of take-home pay, adjusted for your housing costs and your goals — and the single most important thing is that the money leaves your account before you can spend it.

Three common rules, side by side

RuleHow it worksOn $4,000 take-homeBest for
50/30/2050% needs, 30% wants, 20% savings and debt$800 savedStable, moderate housing costs
Pay yourself first, 10%Save 10% before anything else$400 savedGetting started, irregular income
20% savings rateSave 20% regardless of category$800 savedClear goals, higher income

The 50/30/20 split is the most quoted and the most criticised. It works best when housing is under about 30% of take-home pay; in high-rent cities, "needs" alone can exceed 50% and the rule stops being usable. That is not a personal failure — it is a housing-cost problem.

Work backwards from the goal instead

A percentage is arbitrary; a goal is not. Divide the target by the months you have:

Goal6 months12 months24 months36 months
$1,000 emergency buffer$167/mo$83/mo$42/mo$28/mo
$3,000 starter fund$500/mo$250/mo$125/mo$83/mo
$10,000 goal$1,667/mo$833/mo$417/mo$278/mo
$25,000 deposit$4,167/mo$2,083/mo$1,042/mo$694/mo

Working backwards usually produces a number either refreshingly small or brutally large. Both are useful information — the second one tells you to change the deadline or the goal.

When the standard rules do not fit

SituationAdjustment
High housing costsSave what you can; treat rent reduction or income increase as the real lever
Variable incomeSave a percentage of every payment received, not a fixed monthly amount
High-interest debtClearing a 25% APR balance usually beats saving at 4% — do both if you can, minimums plus a small buffer first
No emergency bufferBuild $500–$1,000 first, even at the cost of a lower savings rate this year
Employer retirement matchContribution that gets matched is the highest-return saving available to most people

Make it automatic

The single strongest predictor of saving is whether the transfer happens by itself:

  1. A standing order on payday, not at month end. What is left over at month end is usually nothing.
  2. A separate account, ideally at a different bank and without a card.
  3. Name the accounts by goal — "emergency," "car," "holiday" — because a named goal is harder to raid.
  4. Increase it with every raise, before your spending absorbs the difference. See Lifestyle Creep After a Raise.

Start here

If you have no buffer, target $1,000 fast. If you have some savings and want a target, use How Big Should My Emergency Fund Be?. Then put your monthly figure through the daily savings calculator to see the yearly total and the work hours it represents.

Assumptions: take-home pay, no interest, investment returns, inflation or tax advantages. Employer matching, pension rules and debt interest vary; this is general information, not financial advice.

HOW THESE NUMBERS ARE CALCULATED

Every figure on this page is arithmetic from stated assumptions: take-home pay, an 8-hour working day and 2,080 working hours a year (40 hours × 52 weeks), unless stated otherwise.
