Cost Per Use: The Only Fair Way to Compare Two Purchases

How to compare purchases on cost per use, with tables for shoes, coats and appliances, a break-even test, and when cost per use is the wrong metric.

Two purchases are only comparable on price if they last the same amount of time. Cost per use = price ÷ number of times you use it. A $150 pair of shoes worn 150 times costs $1 per wear; a $60 pair worn 30 times costs $2 per wear — the "cheaper" pair is twice as expensive.

The formula


Cost per use = total cost of ownership ÷ number of uses
Cost per year = total cost of ownership ÷ years of life
Total cost of ownership = purchase price + running costs − resale value

When cost per use is the wrong metric

  • One-off experiences. A concert ticket has one use and that is the point.
  • Things you might not like. Buying the cheap version first is sensible when you are unsure — commitment is a real risk.
  • Rapidly improving categories. A laptop bought to last eight years will be obsolete in five; pay for the horizon you actually have.
  • When quality affects safety or health. Helmets, car seats, tools and footwear are not the place to optimise the last 20%.
  • When the cheap option creates friction. A tool you avoid using because it is unpleasant has a cost per use of infinity.

A 30-second rule

Estimate how many times you will use it in a year, how many years it will last, then divide. If the number is under $1, the decision rarely deserves more thought. If it is over $10, sleep on it — see Seven Questions Before a Big Purchase.

Assumptions: no resale value unless stated, no inflation, no financing costs. Frequency estimates are the reader's to make; overestimating use is the most common error.
