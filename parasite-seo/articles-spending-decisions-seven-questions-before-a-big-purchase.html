Seven Questions to Ask Before Any Big Purchase

A pre-purchase checklist covering cost per use, total cost of ownership, hours of work, opportunity cost and the 30-day test, with a worked example.

A big purchase is any purchase you would notice losing. Seven questions, answered honestly, resolve most of them — and they take about ten minutes.

2. What is the total cost of ownership?

Add everything the purchase commits you to:

Cost typeExamples
Purchase priceIncluding tax and delivery
Running costsEnergy, fuel, consumables, servicing
Recurring costsSubscriptions, insurance, licences
AccessoriesThe things it needs to be usable
End costsDisposal, resale fees, early-termination

A $1,200 laptop with $60 of software a year and a $90 replacement battery in year three is $1,470 over three years.

4. What does this displace?

Money spent here is money not spent elsewhere. Name the alternative: three months of a hobby, a week's holiday, two months of the mortgage, or the emergency fund's next $1,000.

PurchaseEquivalent to
$1,200 phone48 hours of work at $25/h, or a weekend away twice a year
$2,500 laptop2.5 months of a $1,000/mo rent, or 100 hours of work
$9,000 car6 months of a $1,500/mo housing cost, or 360 hours

5. Would I buy it again at this price in a year?

The strongest single test. If the honest answer is no, the purchase is driven by novelty, urgency or pressure rather than by use value.

6. What is the return and repair window?

CheckWhy
Return window lengthA 14-day window and a 90-day window produce very different decisions
Restocking feesCan make a return uneconomic
Warranty length and termsWhat is covered, and who pays shipping
Repairability and spare partsDetermines whether year four is a repair or a replacement

7. Can I wait 30 days?

RuleApply toEffect
24 hoursUnder a day's take-home payCuts novelty buys
7 daysAround a week's payAllows comparison shopping
30 daysA month's pay or moreRemoves urgency as a factor

Waiting is free for most purchases and expensive for none — with the genuine exception of time-limited needs. A purchase that survives 30 days of thinking is usually one you will use.

Worked example: a $1,400 camera

QuestionAnswer
Hours of work56 h at $25/h take-home
Total cost of ownership$1,400 + $250 lens + $80 bag + $60 insurance/yr = ~$1,970 over 3 years
Cost per use40 shoots a year × 3 = 120 → $16.42
What it displacesTwo-thirds of a $3,000 holiday fund
Buy again in a year?Yes — photography is an established hobby
Return window30 days, free returns
Wait 30 days?Yes; price unchanged, decision confirmed

Verdict: buy — because the cost per use is justified by an existing habit, not a hoped-for one.

Use it as a checklist

Copy the seven questions into a note before your next large purchase. For the smaller ones, the cost-of-time calculator answers question 1 in a few seconds, and the 24-hour rule guide covers question 7.

Assumptions: take-home pay, no financing costs, no inflation. Cost-per-use figures depend on honest estimates of frequency.

HOW THESE NUMBERS ARE CALCULATED

Every figure on this page is arithmetic from stated assumptions: take-home pay, an 8-hour working day and 2,080 working hours a year (40 hours × 52 weeks), unless stated otherwise.
