Gross Pay vs Take-Home Pay: What Actually Comes Off Your Salary

The difference between gross and take-home pay, what is deducted in between, and why using gross pay makes every purchase look cheaper than it is.

Gross pay is what your employer agrees to pay you. Take-home pay is what reaches your bank account after tax, social contributions and deductions. The gap between them is often 20% to 40%, and it is the reason a salary figure can be a misleading way to think about affordability.

What typically comes off

Some deductions come out before income tax is calculated (often called pre-tax or salary-sacrifice), which reduces taxable pay — a useful distinction when comparing two job offers with the same headline salary.

The mistake this causes

If you price your spending in gross pay, every purchase looks cheaper than it is:

PurchaseHours at $28.85 grossHours at $20.77 take-homeUnderestimate
$1003.54.81.3 h
$50017.324.16.8 h
$1,500 rent52.072.220.2 h
$5,400 car/year187.2259.972.7 h

A $1,500 rent looks like 52 hours of work in gross terms and is really 72 hours — a difference of two and a half working days a month. Any "hours of work" calculation should use take-home pay.

How to find your own numbers

  1. Take-home: add up the net amounts from your last 12 payslips, or work from a recent month and multiply — being careful with months that have bonuses, overtime or irregular deductions.
  2. Effective deduction rate: 1 − (take-home ÷ gross). If you take home $43,200 of $60,000, the rate is 28%.
  3. Real hourly rate: take-home ÷ hours actually worked, including commute and unpaid time. See Your Real Hourly Wage.

When gross and net move differently

Two things confuse people comparing pay over time:

  • A raise can push part of your income into a higher band without reducing take-home pay; only the portion above the threshold is taxed at the higher rate.
  • Pre-tax deductions reduce taxable pay, so increasing a pension contribution can raise take-home pay by less than the contribution amount.

Assumptions: flat illustrative deduction rates, no bonuses, overtime or variable pay. Tax, social contributions and pension treatment vary substantially by country. General information, not tax or financial advice.
