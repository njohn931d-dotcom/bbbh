The Cost of Time: What an Hour Is Worth

A price is an amount of money. The same price is also an amount of your life, and the second framing is the more useful one.

Updated 2026-09-27 · free, no sign-up

Work hours

Hours = price ÷ take-home hourly rate

Annual take-home divided by 2,080 hours, or monthly by 173.3.

Worked example

Assumptions: Take-home hourly rates of $15, $25, $40 and $80.

What the numbers do not tell you

  • The calculation is deliberately blunt. Its value is that it makes a vague discomfort into a number you can compare against something else.
  • Apply it to purchases you already regret, not to everything. A cost-of-time lens applied universally produces paralysis rather than decisions.
  • At $80 an hour, almost nothing is expensive. At $15 an hour, almost everything is. The tool tells you which of those situations you are in — and that the answer depends on income, not willpower.

Before you rely on this

This is perspective, not an affordability test. Your take-home pay is not all discretionary money.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my real hourly rate?

Take annual take-home pay, divide by the hours you actually work in a year including unpaid extras, and use that. Including benefits gives a fairer figure for comparing offers.

Is converting prices to work hours useful?

As a way of noticing patterns, yes. As a budgeting method, no — your take-home pay goes to rent, food and saving, so treating all of it as available time overstates what a purchase costs.

Should high earners use a higher valuation for their time?

Arguably yes. The opportunity cost of a senior person’s hour is higher than the hourly wage, and the work-hours framing understates it.
