Price to Hours: The Formula for Turning Money Into Work Time

The complete price-to-hours formula: hourly, monthly and annual inputs, the pre-tax adjustment, recurring costs, and the five mistakes that skew it.

Take the price, divide it by your take-home hourly pay, and you have the hours of work the price represents. Hours = price ÷ take-home hourly pay. Everything else on this page is about getting the two inputs right.

Step 1: find your take-home hourly pay

You have three ways in, depending on what you know:

You knowDivide byGives
Hourly take-home rate—Use it directly
Monthly take-home pay173.33Hourly (40 h/week)
Annual take-home pay2,080Hourly (40 h/week)

The 173.33 and 2,080 divisors come from a 40-hour week over 52 weeks. If you work 35 hours a week, use 151.67 and 1,820 instead. If you work 50, use 216.67 and 2,600.

Using gross pay instead of take-home pay is the single most common error. It makes every purchase look a fifth to a third cheaper than it is.

Step 2: apply the formula


Hours of work = price ÷ take-home hourly pay

A $240 dinner at $30 an hour take-home is 8 hours. The same dinner at $60 an hour is 4 hours.

Step 3: adjust for tax when comparing to gross pay

When you compare a price to a salary figure, or when the price is paid out of pre-tax income, convert first:


Gross needed = price ÷ (1 − effective tax rate)
Hours = gross needed ÷ gross hourly rate

Step 5: handle recurring costs

Recurring costs are where this lens matters most, because the annual number is invisible on a monthly statement.


Annual hours = (monthly cost × 12) ÷ hourly pay
Yearly-daily habit hours = (daily cost × 365) ÷ hourly pay

Five mistakes that make the number wrong

  1. Gross instead of take-home. Understates hours by roughly the tax rate.
  2. Contract hours instead of real hours. Ignores commute, overtime and unpaid admin; understates hours.
  3. Ignoring the lifetime of the item. A $400 coat worn for eight winters is $50 a year; a $100 coat worn for one is not cheaper.
  4. Forgetting the running costs. A printer for $80 with $40 cartridges is not an $80 purchase.
  5. Treating the result as a verdict. The number shows the trade-off. It does not tell you whether the trade is worth making — only you know what the purchase is for.

Do it automatically

The cost-of-time calculator takes a price and an hourly, monthly or annual pay figure and returns hours, with the 2,080-hour year assumed. For recurring costs, use the subscription calculator (monthly × 12) or the daily savings calculator (daily × 365).

Assumptions: take-home pay, 40-hour weeks, 2,080 hours a year, constant prices and pay. Not included: inflation, interest, investment returns, or taxes beyond the stated effective rate.

HOW THESE NUMBERS ARE CALCULATED

Every figure on this page is arithmetic from stated assumptions: take-home pay, an 8-hour working day and 2,080 working hours a year (40 hours × 52 weeks), unless stated otherwise.
