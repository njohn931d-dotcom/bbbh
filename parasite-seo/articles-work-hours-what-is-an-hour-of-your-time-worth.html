What Is an Hour of Your Time Worth? (Formula + Table)

Work out what one hour of your time is worth using take-home pay and hours actually worked, with a table from $15 to $100 per hour and the mistakes to avoid.

An hour of your time is worth your take-home pay divided by the hours you actually work. If you take home $52,000 a year for 2,080 hours of work, one hour is worth $25. That single number is what turns a price tag into hours of your life.

The formula is simple. Getting a useful answer depends on two choices: which pay figure you use, and how many hours you count.

The basic formula


Hourly value = take-home pay ÷ hours worked

Take-home pay means the money that actually reaches your bank account after tax, deductions and pension contributions. Hours worked means the time you genuinely give up — not just the hours on your contract.

These figures assume 2,080 working hours a year (40 hours × 52 weeks). If your schedule differs, divide by your own hours instead.

Why gross pay gives the wrong answer

Dividing your salary by 2,080 uses money you never receive. Tax, social contributions and deductions can remove a fifth to a third of a gross salary before it arrives. If you spend take-home money but calculate with gross pay, every purchase looks cheaper than it really is — sometimes by a third.

Why "hours worked" is not the same as "hours contracted"

A 40-hour contract plus a 45-minute daily commute, 30 minutes of getting-ready time and two hours of weekend email is closer to a 50-hour week. Over 48 working weeks, that is 2,400 hours rather than 2,080. Someone earning $52,000 take-home on that schedule is not earning $25 an hour — they are earning about $21.70.

The honest version of this calculation is uncomfortable on purpose. It counts the time you actually lose, not the time you are paid for.

Three ways people use the number

  1. A spending threshold. Some people set a rule: anything under one hour of work needs no deliberation, anything over a day of work gets a night's sleep first — the version we use in Seven Questions Before a Big Purchase.
  2. Comparing two purchases. A $200 item that lasts five years and a $60 item you replace every year are not comparable on price. Divide by how long it lasts.
  3. Deciding whether to outsource. If a task takes you three hours and you can pay someone $40 to do it, the arithmetic favours outsourcing when your hour is worth more than $13.33.

What the number cannot tell you

It is not an affordability test. Your take-home pay also has to cover rent, food, debt and savings, so "four hours of work" does not mean "four hours of disposable money." It is also not a moral verdict: a purchase that costs a week of work and delivers a year of daily use can be the best money you spend all year.

The number is a lens, not a rule. Use it to make the trade-off visible, then decide — and if you want the full derivation, including the pre-tax adjustment and the recurring-cost version, read Price to Hours: The Formula.

Where this figure comes from

The 2,080-hour year is 40 hours × 52 weeks with no unpaid leave. Paid vacation days reduce the hours you work without reducing pay, which raises your real hourly value — divide your take-home pay by your actual hours to capture that.
