How Big Should My Emergency Fund Be? Sizing by Your Numbers

Size an emergency fund from 1 to 12 months of essential expenses, with tables by income and job stability, and a two-phase plan for building one.

The most common guideline is three to six months of essential expenses. The right end of that range depends on how stable your income is, how many people rely on it, and how quickly you could find work again.

The size is measured in essential expenses, not income — the money you would still spend if you stopped earning: housing, food, utilities, transport, insurance, medication, minimum debt payments.

Size by situation

SituationSuggested months of essentialsWhy
Salaried, stable sector, no dependents3Income risk is low
Salaried, one income supporting a household6One income, multiple dependents
Freelance or contract6 – 9Income gaps are normal, not emergencies
Commission or seasonal income9 – 12Slow periods recur annually
Single-income household with dependents6 – 12No second earner to absorb a gap
Business owner, variable drawings9 – 12Business and personal risk overlap
Between jobs or on a fixed-term contract6 – 12The end date is known

Two phases: the buffer, then the fund

Going straight for $16,800 is where most people stall. Split it:

PhaseTargetPurposeTypical timeline
1: Starter buffer$500 – $1,000Absorbs a repair, a dentist bill, a shortfall4 – 12 weeks
2: Full fund3 – 6 months of essentialsCovers job loss or illness12 – 36 months

The starter buffer is what stops a $600 car repair from becoming credit card debt, which is exactly how a full fund never gets built. See How to Save $1,000 Fast for phase one.

Where to keep it

FeatureWhy it matters
Instant accessAn emergency is not planned a week ahead
Separate from your current accountMoney you can see is money you spend
Insured up to your local protection limitCheck the scheme and limit where you live
No volatilityThe fund must be the same size in a bad month as a good one
No notice periodNotice accounts delay access precisely when you need it

An emergency fund is not an investment. Its job is to be boring, stable and available — which means accepting a low return in exchange for certainty.

What counts as an emergency

Usually yesUsually no
Job loss or reduced hoursA sale you do not want to miss
Urgent medical or dental costsA holiday, however well-deserved
Essential appliance or car failureA non-essential upgrade
Unexpected travel for a family emergencyRoutine maintenance you could have planned for
Emergency housing repairGifts

The test: is it unexpected, necessary, and urgent? Two out of three is usually a sinking fund, not an emergency — and planning for those in advance is what keeps this fund intact.

Building it alongside debt

Debt typeTypical approach
High-interest revolving debtBuild a small buffer ($500–$1,000), then prioritise the debt
Low-interest or 0% debtSplit contributions between fund and debt
Student or mortgage debtKeep building the fund; these are long-term, low-rate
Employer-matched retirement contributionTake the match first; it usually beats everything else

Refill rules

If you use the fund, it becomes the priority again. Two habits help: pause non-essential saving until it is restored, and keep a written note of what it is for — the note is what makes "no" easy when a non-emergency comes along.

Assumptions: no interest, investment returns, inflation or tax treatment. Deposit insurance limits and unemployment protections vary by country. General information, not financial advice.
