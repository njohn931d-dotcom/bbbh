How Much House Can You Afford?

Lenders will approve more than most people can comfortably pay. This is the arithmetic behind the number they quote and the number you should use.

Updated 2026-09-27 · free, no sign-up

Lender 28/36 rule

Max payment = 0.36 × gross monthly income

Housing costs (P&I, tax, insurance) should stay under 36% of gross monthly income, with tax and insurance under 28% on their own.

Worked example

Assumptions: A household earning $120,000 a year, $9,000 in liquid savings, no debts over $1,000 a month.

What the numbers do not tell you

  • Cash costs buyers routinely forget: down payment, closing costs of roughly 2–5% of the price, and moving expenses. On a $400,000 house that is $40,000 plus about $12,000 of closing costs before you have made a single payment.
  • The 36% rule measures housing against income before retirement savings. If you are behind on retirement contributions, add them back before applying the ratio, or the rule understates your true cost.
  • Stretching to the maximum approval leaves nothing for repairs, a car, or a lost job. A payment you can cover on a reduced schedule is worth more than the extra square footage.

Before you rely on this

Qualification rules differ by lender and by loan program. A mortgage pre-approval from two or three lenders is the only reliable answer.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 28/36 rule?

It is a lender guideline, not a law. Housing costs should stay under 28% of gross monthly income, and total debt payments under 36%. Many buyers find 36% uncomfortable in practice and target 30% or less.

How much do I need for a down payment?

Conventional loans want 20% to avoid private mortgage insurance, though 3–10% is widely accepted. First-time buyer programs may allow 3% with additional assistance.

Should I buy a house as soon as I can afford it?

Not necessarily. Renting is not throwing money away: it is buying optionality. The case for buying gets stronger when you have a stable income, a multi-year horizon, and a payment comfortably below the stress-test line.
