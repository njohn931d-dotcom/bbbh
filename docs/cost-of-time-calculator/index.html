Turn Any Price Into Hours of Your Life

The cost-of-time method: converting purchase prices into hours of work to judge whether something is truly worth buying.

September 27, 2026

Converting a price into hours of your life is the simplest perspective trick in personal finance: divide what something costs by what you actually earn per hour — after tax — and the result tells you how much of your finite working time the purchase consumes. A $150 pair of shoes at $25/hour costs six hours of your life. The math is trivial; the effect on decision-making is not.

Why hours hit harder than dollars

Dollars are abstract and infinitely reusable in the imagination — there's always "more money." Time is none of those things: everyone gets the same 168 hours a week, and working hours exchange only once, for money you've already agreed to trade. Price-in-dollars activates the merchant's frame (what you part with); price-in-hours activates your frame (what you give up). Behavioral research on unit framing supports what most people discover anecdotally: the same number in a personally meaningful unit changes the decision.

The technique is sometimes called the "hours of freedom" calculation or the cost-of-time method — the name matters less than the habit: never look at a price tag without converting it.

The formula

  1. Net hourly rate = after-tax monthly income ÷ actual hours worked that month (or use take-home annual ÷ 2,080 for a standard 40-hour week).
  2. Hours of life = price ÷ net hourly rate.
  3. Optional annualization: recurring daily or weekly costs × 365 or 52 first — then convert.

Example: $2,400 take-home per month over 173 working hours ≈ $13.87/hour. That $900 phone = ~65 hours ≈ eight working days. The "eight days of life" framing does work that "$900" alone rarely does.

Two refinements matter:

  • Use take-home pay, not salary. Gross pay inflates your rate by 20–35% and makes every purchase look cheaper than it is.
  • Use your real hours, not the standard week. If unpaid overtime inflates your actual week, your true hourly rate is lower and the hours-of-life number is higher. Some people prefer the opposite: use only hours they freely choose to work, making the number sting appropriately.

Where to apply it

  • Impulse and mid-size buys: the sweet spot — clothing, gadgets, dinners, upgrades. Any purchase where the decision is genuinely open.
  • Recurring costs: a $45 monthly subscription is 3.25 hours of work every month — 39 hours a year. Subscriptions rarely survive their hours-of-life review.
  • Lifestyle decisions: a $300/month nicer apartment = 21.6 extra hours monthly. Sometimes it's worth every minute; making the comparison explicitly is the point.
  • Salary and side-gig decisions: the mirror version — compute what your free time is worth when deciding whether overtime or a second job is "worth it."

Where it misleads

  • Time-valuation guilt. Applying hours-of-life to everything turns joy into arithmetic. Use it as a pre-commitment filter for open decisions, not a verdict on expenses you've already chosen deliberately and love.
  • It ignores investment returns. The dollars saved could compound; the hours spent cannot. For big purchases, run both lenses.
  • Zero-hour periods. In a genuine cash crunch, protect essentials first; perspective math is a decision tool, not a reason to skip rent.

The disciplined version: convert the price, name what the hours could do otherwise (a day off, the trip fund growing, debt shrinking), then decide. If the purchase still wins — buy it and enjoy it without the residue of guilt. That's the entire goal: fewer reflex purchases, zero penalized deliberate ones.

Frequently asked questions

What hourly rate should I use?

After-tax income divided by hours actually worked. Monthly take-home ÷ 173 hours is the standard approximation for a full-time month; adjust if your hours differ. The most honest version uses your real week, unpaid overtime included.

Does this method work for large purchases like cars?

Yes, but pair it with total-cost math — insurance, fuel, depreciation — since a car is a stream of costs, not one price. The hours-of-life figure on the monthly running cost is often the more revealing number.

Why do some people dislike this technique?

Because relentless conversion can make all spending feel like self-harm. The fix is scoping: apply it to decisions, not to enjoyed outcomes. A deliberate vacation priced in hours and approved in hours is still a vacation.
