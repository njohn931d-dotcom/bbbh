The Sunk Cost Fallacy and Your Money

What the sunk cost fallacy is, how it traps spending on things that already failed, and how to decide forward instead of backward.

September 27, 2026

The sunk cost fallacy is the tendency to keep investing in something — money, time, effort — because of what you've already put in, rather than whether future investment makes sense. Sunk costs are gone regardless of what you do next; basing decisions on them is letting the past spend your future.

Sunk costs in plain English

A sunk cost is any resource already spent and unrecoverable: the non-refundable retreat, the concert tickets, the year of a degree you've stopped believing in, the subscription renewed while you "should" use it. Rational decisions compare only future costs against future benefits. Emotional brains add the past ledger — and the past ledger always argues for continuing.

That's why the pattern repeats: I've come too far to quit, I've already watched three episodes, I've paid for the year. Each sentence is an accounting error.

The classic money examples

  • Entertainment already paid for. Tickets bought, plans changed — going anyway when you'd rather stay home means paying twice: the money, plus the evening.
  • Bad membership, renewed. Annual gym payment is gone whether you go or not; "getting your money's worth" by forcing miserable sessions has negative value versus doing what actually fits.
  • The project that keeps eating cash. The side business, the car repair stacking past the car's worth, the stock you refuse to sell because "I'm down 40%."
  • Careers and degrees. Continuing a path for the sake of years already invested, rather than the next five years' expected return.

Why we fall for it

Several effects reinforce each way: loss aversion makes giving up feel like realizing a loss — quitting converts an abstract sunk cost into a concrete "I wasted it" feeling; commitment escalation protects self-image (I'm not the kind of person who quits); and the finish-line pull makes nearly-done projects feel automatically worth completing.

There's also simple bookkeeping confusion: people treat unrecoverable money as if it were a down payment that future spending can still "cash in." It can't.

The forward test

Replace "have I gotten my money's worth?" with one question:

"Knowing everything I know right now, with zero history — would I start this today?"

If no: stop, sell, cancel, walk away. The past money is gone either way; the only live question is where the next dollar and hour go. This single reframe is the entire cure, and it's reusable in minutes: cancel the unused membership, cut losses on the repair, leave the movie.

Two nuances keep the rule honest:

  • Sunk costs can be information, not obligations. If the failed project taught you the market doesn't want the product, that's evidence for the forward decision — just don't dress it up as repayment.
  • Pure enjoyment isn't a fallacy. Staying at a conference you're hating because you'll see friends tomorrow is a future benefit; decide on that, not the badge price.

A practical cleanup ritual

Quarterly, list anything you fund or attend mainly because of what you've already put in. For each: apply the forward test. Cancel what fails, and write one sentence about why — you'll spot the pattern you're repeating (my renewed-but-unused plan; my repair-that-cost-more-than-the-thing) faster than any budget will show you.

Frequently asked questions

How is sunk cost different from opportunity cost?

Sunk cost is about past spending you can't get back — it should be ignored. Opportunity cost is about the future alternative you're giving up — it should be weighed. Smart decisions ignore the first and compare the second.

Aren't I wasting money if I quit early?

Only if future benefits are zero — and the money is gone regardless. Quitting early doesn't create the loss; it stops future losses. The waste happened at the purchase, not the cancellation.

What about loyalty programs and points?

Treat points like currency you already spent. Sunk points shouldn't justify future spending ("I have miles, so the trip is free") — the trip still costs what it costs in time and cash. Run the forward test on the trip itself.

Does this apply to investments?

Carefully: selling a losing position at a loss feels like confirming a mistake, but the market doesn't know your purchase price. Future returns depend on the asset's prospects, not your basis. (Tax rules and fees are real future considerations — psychology is not one of them.)
